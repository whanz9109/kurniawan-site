Wave and ocean current energy for Indonesian coasts

The south coast of Java receives ocean swell almost all year round. Since 2022 this group has built the full wave energy converter (WEC) chain: mapping the wave resource, shaping the floater, designing the hydraulic power take-off (PTO), and arranging and hardening the devices, with Pelabuhanratu as the case study. Since 2026 the work has extended to floating ocean current power for small islands.

Capturing energy at the surface

Orbital water motion decays quickly with depth, so wave energy is most available near the surface. A floating point absorber uses this: the floater heaves with the waves, and a hydraulic system turns that motion into pressure and motor rotation to generate electricity. The rig shown here is used to test such PTO units in the laboratory.

The research chain from sea to electricity

01

Wave resource

Mapping the wave energy resource in Indonesian waters from hindcast data, with economic feasibility, plus a low-cost smartbuoy prototype for wave measurement.

02

Floater

Shape and size of multi-cylinder floaters optimised for Pelabuhanratu, balancing captured power against cost.

03

Hydraulic PTO

Cylinders, valves, hoses and accumulators designed for steady power output, then tested on a laboratory rig with a pneumatic drive that mimics waves.

04

Arrays & survivability

WEC array configurations and the trade-off between performance and survivability in the waters south of Java.

What comes next

The work is moving from waves to currents: a floating ocean current power plant (FOCPP) prototype with a magnetic-bearing ducted turbine for Indonesia’s small islands, funded by the 2026 UI Innovation Funding programme. Alongside it, MARDEV is preparing a wave flume and towing tank, targeted to open in July 2028, for model tests of WECs and current turbines.
